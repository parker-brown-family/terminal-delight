# Purposes

Choose the job the reader needs done before choosing sections. The same purpose can use HTML or Markdown. A medium is a reading and interaction choice; a purpose is an intended outcome.

| Purpose | The reader must be able to answer | Include when material supports it | Expected response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summary | What happened, what matters, and what follows? | Result, significant change, uncertainty, next action | Understanding or correction; no approval is required by the format |
| Engineering | How does this work, why was it shaped this way, and what establishes that it works? | Mechanism, boundary, alternatives that mattered, validation and limits | Technical correction, acceptance, or a next implementation step |
| Decision | What choice is mine, what do you recommend, and what follows from choosing it? | Options, recommendation, consequences, uncertainty and a concrete ask | A choice, an amendment or deferral |

A summary can contain a decision, and an engineering brief can end in a choice. Name the primary purpose from the reader's job. Add a secondary purpose only when it changes the content. Avoid three complete documents stacked in one file.

An audit, incident explanation or research brief may need a distinct purpose. State the intended reader outcome and use the same evidence and feedback principles. Do not force it through a decision template.

For HTML, a summary often benefits from a before/after view, an engineering brief from a mechanism diagram, and a decision brief from an at-size mockup or comparison. These are drawing prompts, not required section lists.

Use the reader's budget to decide what belongs on the page. Background that only some readers need can be linked evidence. A fact needed to interpret the recommendation belongs in the brief even if it is inconvenient.
